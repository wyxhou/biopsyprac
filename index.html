Theory (introduction)
  • Neuropsychology has been one of the fastest growing domains in psychology since the mid-20th century. Investigations assess structural and functional pathology through radio-diagnostic procedures, and neuropsychological batteries help treatment and rehabilitation.
  • Neuropsychological tests are better than brain scans financially and in the accuracy of predicting the presence or deterioration of psycho-cognitive functioning. They also give a profile of psychological parameters, which helps classification under psychiatric or neurological conditions.
  • Halstead (1947), modified by Reitan (1966), is one of the most popular and sensitive batteries, used to localise brain lesions. Its use in "difficult to diagnose" cases was not shown.
  • The Luria-Nebraska battery is comprehensive, but recent research showed limits, such as few variables to separate psychotics from brain-damaged patients.
  • These tests also assess normal cognitive functions such as memory, intelligence, visual organisation and perceptual-motor skills, including drawing tests. These become cues for brain damage as well.

The PGI Memory Scale

Memory is believed to be a function of the temporal cerebral cortex: left for verbal and right for non-verbal. PGIMS gives a comprehensive and simple measure of:

  • verbal and non-verbal memory, on neurological theory;
  • short-term and long-term memory, on experimental evidence;
  • remote, recent and immediate memory, on clinical practice.

Correlation with Boston's Memory Scale is 0.71 and with Wechsler's Memory Scale 0.85. It has satisfactory cross-validity and gives quantile norms and a profile. Separate norms exist for three education levels: 0 to 5, 6 to 9, and 10 and above years of schooling.

Aim, plan, materials
Aim

To assess the level of dysfunction in the subject using the PGI Memory Scale sub-test of the PGI Battery of Brain Dysfunction.

Plan

  2. A set of 5 cards for visual retention (sub-test IX).
  3. A set of 2 cards: one with pictures of 10 common objects, the second with pictures of 20 common objects for recognition (sub-test X).
  4. A stop-clock.

Procedure
  1. Seat the subject comfortably in a well-ventilated and well-lit room. Establish rapport.
  2. Present sub-tests I to X one by one with clear instructions. Administration takes about 15 to 20 minutes.
  3. Follow the broad hints of administration in the test blanks.
  4. Special care: reliability of responses on the first 2 sub-tests; the rate of presentation of digits, words and pairs on sub-tests IV, V, VII and VIII; and the interval between presenting material and asking for recall.
  5. Score the responses and interpret dysfunction using the norms.
Instructions to the subject (journal p.15 and p.16)

I and II, Remote and Recent memory: "Here are two sub-tests with questions related to your life, you should try to answer all of them with as much accuracy as possible."

III, Mental balance: "I am going to ask you to perform a few tasks like reciting alphabets or counting numbers with certain rules. You have to do the same with the best of your effort."

IV, Attention and concentration. Forward: "I am going to call out sets of numbers, listen to them carefully and repeat them in the same order as I presented it to you." Backward: "I am going to call out sets of numbers, listen to them carefully and repeat them backward. E.g.: if I say 2, 5 you must say 5, 2."

V, Delayed recall: "I am going to read the names of some objects, listen carefully, and when I ask you to repeat, you should do so in the same order."

VI, Immediate recall: "I am going to read a few small sentences one by one, listen carefully, because when I am done, I would like you to tell me the whole sentence as precisely as you can."

VII, Similar pairs: "I am going to read to you a list of pairs, i.e. two words at a time. Listen carefully, when I name one word of the pair, you have to tell me the second word of that pair."

VIII, Dissimilar pairs: instructions and procedure same as above.

IX, Visual retention: "I am going to show you a card. Observe it carefully. After some time, I will take it away, and when I ask you to draw it, you must draw it on the paper with the help of your memory."

X, Recognition: "I will show you a card containing pictures of many objects. See the whole card attentively. After some time, I will place before you another card. From this card you will be required to identify and name the objects that you saw in the previous card."

Scoring and interpretation
  1. Score the responses with the help of the key and manual.
  2. Add the total score for each sub-test.
  3. Transcribe the total scores into a converted score (from the norms for the subject's education level).
  4. Use the converted scores to get the dysfunction rating.
  5. The maximum rating for each sub-test is 3, so 30 (3 × 10) for the whole PGIMS.

Theory (introduction)

Mind-body relation

  • One's thoughts and emotions play a central role in all aspects of health. Looking after our psychological well-being can prevent medical illness and often speeds recovery.
  • Attitudes, beliefs and emotional states, from love and compassion to fear and anger, trigger chain reactions that affect blood chemistry, heart rate and the activity of every cell and organ, from the stomach and digestive tract to the immune system.
  • Many therapies use the mind-body connection: stress management, support groups, individual psychotherapy, biofeedback, hypnosis and relaxation techniques.
  • Psycho-physiological methods are a subset of neuroscience methods. Many neuroscience methods are invasive. Measures include heart rate and blood pressure, and techniques such as TMS, PET, MEG, EEG, structural MRI and fMRI. Questionnaires include the Health Assessment Questionnaire, Gameplay Experience Questionnaire and Berkeley Expressivity Questionnaire.
  • Awareness of the mind-body connection helps with stress, weight control, smoking cessation, substance abuse, chronic pain and fatigue. It can reduce missed work, speed recovery, reduce pain, shorten hospital stays, help patients and families cope, and increase mental alertness.

About the inventory

  • As diagnosis and treatment of somatic disease became more precise, Objective Psychological Tests (OPTs) were developed to help non-psychiatric physicians define emotional states and attitudes more precisely.
  • Psychoneurotic disorders are mental disorders without any demonstrable organic basis. The person may have considerable insight, does not confuse morbid subjective experiences and fantasies with external reality, behaviour stays within socially acceptable limits and personality is not disorganised. Main signs: excessive anxiety, hysterical symptoms, phobias, obsessional and compulsive symptoms, depression.
  • The questions come from an investigation of the psychological and physiological experiences that separate the "psychoneurotic" from the "normal" individual. They are grouped by whether they seem mainly "mental" or "physical".
  • Designed for late adolescents or adults of either gender. Physicians, clinicians, social workers and school psychologists use it to detect neurotic tendencies. The division lets the user see whether the main dysfunction is psychological or physiological.
  • The questionnaire method has limited value because there is no clear-cut clinical description of a psychoneurotic, and because of the nature of the test. An inventory built on empirical procedures with patients separates clinician-diagnosed patients from supposedly normal subjects. Depending on the subject's cooperation, it can also detect people in the normal group who might profit from psychiatric guidance or treatment.

Procedure

The inventory is given to the subject, who is told to read the items carefully and select one of the four alternatives. The subject answers the questions in Part I and Part II. The inventory is usually completed in 20 minutes. It is scored and interpreted later.

Instructions to the subject (journal p.35)

"This inventory has questions about your subjective experiences. Read each statement carefully and select responses from the four alternatives provided: 'Often', 'Sometimes', 'Seldom' and 'Never'. Make sure you select only one of the four alternatives provided. There is no right or wrong answer."

Scoring

Scoring is done with stencils. Add the weight for each answer as printed on the keys. Where there is no number on the key, the weight is 0 and need not be recorded.

  1. A score of Part I is obtained (physiological dysfunction).
  2. A score of Part II is obtained (psychological dysfunction).
  3. A total score is obtained (Part I + Part II).
  4. Subtract 176 from the raw score on Part I and 249 from the raw score on Part II.
  5. Subtract 425 from the total raw score to get the final score.
  6. Convert each final score into its corresponding sten score (and percentile) from the norms.

Critical Sten Score (CSS) = 4. A sten score below 4 is a "serious" case and should be referred to a clinician.

Theory (introduction)
  • Constructed by H.J. Eysenck and Sybil B.G. Eysenck as a development of the Maudsley Personality Inventory (MPI).
  • Advantages over the MPI: (i) two parallel forms, A and B, so retesting is possible; (ii) a lie scale to check social desirability; (iii) items reworded to suit subjects with less than average intelligence.
  • In the MPI there was a small correlation between extraversion (E) and neuroticism (N). In the EPQ only suitable items were kept so the correlation is removed. Retest reliability is higher than the MPI (around 0.85), and there is direct evidence of validity.

Theoretical rationale

  • According to Eysenck, personality is dimensional. One dimension is extraversion-introversion, another is neuroticism versus normality or emotional stability. Later research added a third, psychoticism.
  • Neuroticism is closely related to the degree of liability of the autonomic nervous system. Extraversion is closely related to the balance of excitation or inhibition in the central nervous system, mediated through the reticular activating system.
  • Based on E and N, Eysenck plotted individuals into 4 quadrants.

Extravert

Sociable, likes parties, has many friends, needs people to talk to and craves excitement. Likes taking chances, is generally impulsive and tends to lose temper quickly. Not always a reliable person.

Introvert

Quiet, retiring, introspective, fond of books rather than people. Keeps feelings under control, seldom aggressive, does not lose temper easily. Reliable, somewhat pessimistic, places great emphasis on ethical standards.

Neuroticism

Emotionally unstable, easily aroused, moody, touchy, anxious and restless. Also unreliable.

Emotional stability

Emotionally stable, less easily aroused, calm, even-tempered, carefree and reliable.

Psychoticism

Usually characterised by loss or distortion of reality and the inability to tell reality from fantasy. A psychotic person may have disturbances in thought, emotion and motor behaviour, and delusions or hallucinations. The factor also includes some degree of psychopathy: anti-social and impulsive behaviour, egocentrism and absence of guilt.

Unlike E-I and N-stability, psychoticism is not a dimension with an opposite pole. It is an ingredient present in varying degrees in individuals. Eysenck (1975) lists 11 characteristics of high scorers:

  • solitary, not caring for people
  • troublesome, not fitting in
  • cruel, inhuman
  • lack feeling, insensitive
  • sensation-seeking, under-aroused
  • hostile to others, aggressive
  • like odd, unusual things
  • disregard danger, foolhardy
  • like to make fools of other people, upsetting them
  • opposed to accepted social customs
  • engage in little personal interaction, prefer "impersonal" sex

Eysenck's studies suggest psychotic disorders are very different from neurotic disorders. There are different routes to each, so a person may become increasingly neurotic without being psychotic.

(b) Extraversion. Total raw score 127, mean 12.7, sten 5.6, 'Average'. The centile rank of 52 implies that about 52 score below this group and about 47 above. Individual differences: S1, S5, S9 have 'High' extraversion and S8 has 'Low' extraversion.

(c) Neuroticism. Total raw score 100, mean 10.0, sten 4.4, 'Average'. The centile rank of 29.1 implies that about 29 score below this group and about 70 above. Individual differences: S4, S8 show 'Low' neuroticism and no member shows 'High'.

(d) Lie score. Total lie score 89, mean 8.9, sten 6.3, centile rank 65.5, 'Average'. The group lie score is average, so the responses can be taken at face value. S6, S8 have 'High' lie scores, so their other scores must be read with caution.

Conclusion

  • The group on the whole is 'Average' across all four areas.
  • Individual differences exist within the group.

Verbal instructions

Learn each script word for word. The words below are from your journal.

1. PGIMS (ten short scripts)

I and II, Remote and Recent: "Here are two sub-tests with questions related to your life, you should try to answer all of them with as much accuracy as possible."

III, Mental balance: "I am going to ask you to perform a few tasks like reciting alphabets or counting numbers with certain rules. You have to do the same with the best of your effort."

IV, Forward: "I am going to call out sets of numbers, listen to them carefully and repeat them in the same order as I presented it to you." Backward: "I am going to call out sets of numbers, listen to them carefully and repeat them backward. E.g.: if I say 2, 5 you must say 5, 2."

V, Delayed recall: "I am going to read the names of some objects, listen carefully, and when I ask you to repeat, you should do so in the same order."

VI, Immediate recall: "I am going to read a few small sentences one by one, listen carefully, because when I am done, I would like you to tell me the whole sentence as precisely as you can."

VII, Similar pairs: "I am going to read to you a list of pairs, i.e. two words at a time. Listen carefully, when I name one word of the pair, you have to tell me the second word of that pair."

VIII, Dissimilar pairs: same as VII.

IX, Visual retention: "I am going to show you a card. Observe it carefully. After some time, I will take it away, and when I ask you to draw it, you must draw it on the paper with the help of your memory."

X, Recognition: "I will show you a card containing pictures of many objects. See the whole card attentively. After some time, I will place before you another card. From this card you will be required to identify and name the objects that you saw in the previous card."

From your journal: well-ventilated, well-lit room, with rapport. Sub-tests I to X are presented one by one with clear instructions, in about 15 to 20 minutes. Special care goes to the first 2 sub-tests, the rate of presentation of digits, words and pairs (IV, V, VII, VIII), and the interval between presenting the material and asking for recall.

2. PPSI

"This inventory has questions about your subjective experiences. Read each statement carefully and select responses from the four alternatives provided: 'Often', 'Sometimes', 'Seldom' and 'Never'. Make sure you select only one of the four alternatives provided. There is no right or wrong answer."

From your journal: the subject reads the items carefully and selects one of the four alternatives, in Part I and Part II. Usually completed in 20 minutes, then scored and interpreted.

3. EPQ

"There are few questions regarding the way you feel and act. After each question, there is a space provided with options 'YES' or 'NO'. Read each question carefully and mark either 'YES' or 'NO' as your response. Do not spend too much time on any one question and give your first response."

From your journal: the subject is told to work as quickly as possible, and to make sure all questions are answered. The subject is assured of the confidentiality of the answers. For literates the instructions are printed on the form. For an illiterate subject the questions are read out and the answers recorded. The booklet is taken back after completion and scored.

Answer in two or three sentences, then stop.
General
What is biological psychology?

The application of the principles of biology to the study of mental processes and behaviour, that is, psychology in terms of bodily mechanisms. Its basic assumption is that psychological processes have biological (physiological) correlates.

What is neuropsychological assessment?

A comprehensive assessment of cognitive processes: attention and concentration, verbal and visual memory, visual-spatial functioning, language, executive functioning, emotional and personality development, and more.

Why use neuropsychological tests when brain scans exist?

Your journal gives two reasons: they are cheaper, and they predict the presence or deterioration of psycho-cognitive functioning accurately. They also give a profile of psychological parameters that helps classify a case as psychiatric or neurological.

What is the biological theory of personality?

Genetic makeup determines personality at least in part. Temperament, extraversion and introversion have strong biological links. Hans Eysenck is the best-known biological theorist of personality. Inheritance is only partially correct, and environment cannot be ignored.

Standardised test, norms, raw score, sten, centile?

A standardised test has fixed instructions and scoring. Norms are the scores of a reference group. Raw score is the number of scored items. Sten and centile show where that raw score falls against the norms. extra

Why are confidentiality and rapport important?

The subject answers about personal experiences. Confidentiality and a relaxed subject give honest, reliable responses.

PGIMS
What is the PGI Battery of Brain Dysfunction (PGI-BBD)?

A neuropsychological battery developed by the Department of Psychiatry, Post-Graduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGIMER), Chandigarh.

Name its five sub-tests and variables.

PGI Memory Scale (10 variables), Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence (2), Verbal Adult Intelligence Scale (5), Nahor-Benson Test (1), Bender Visual Motor Gestalt Test (1). Total 19 variables.

Who was it standardised on, and how long does it take?

Adult males and females, literate and illiterate, aged 20 to 45 years (usable up to 50). About 90 to 120 minutes to administer. Scoring takes about 30 minutes at first and 5 to 7 minutes with experience.

What does PGI-BBD measure?

Intelligence (verbal and non-verbal), memory, perceptual activity and transference from one hemisphere to the other.

Uses of the battery?

Diagnosis or assessment of schizophrenia, epilepsy, mania, head injury and substance abuse; evaluating loss or decline in cognitive functions; planning rehabilitation; helping the judiciary decide compensation; planning treatment or training; evaluating outcome of treatment.

Multi-factorial vs unitary approach?

Multi-factorial is based on the differential functions of the brain (Luria-Nebraska, NIMHANS battery). Unitary is based on the functional unity of the brain (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, Trail-Making Test).

Halstead-Reitan and Luria-Nebraska batteries?

Halstead (1947), modified by Reitan (1966), is one of the most popular and sensitive batteries, used to localise brain lesions, though its use in difficult-to-diagnose cases was not shown. Luria-Nebraska is comprehensive, but recent research showed limits, such as too few variables to separate psychotics from brain-damaged patients.

What does PGIMS measure, and which brain area is linked to memory?

Verbal and non-verbal memory (neurological theory), short-term and long-term memory (experimental evidence), and remote, recent and immediate memory (clinical practice). Memory is a function of the temporal cerebral cortex, left for verbal and right for non-verbal.

List the 10 sub-tests.

I Remote memory, II Recent memory, III Mental balance, IV Attention and concentration, V Delayed recall, VI Immediate recall, VII Verbal retention for similar pairs, VIII Verbal retention for dissimilar pairs, IX Visual retention, X Recognition.

How reliable and valid is PGIMS?

Correlation with Boston's Memory Scale is 0.71 and with Wechsler's Memory Scale 0.85 (your journal). It has satisfactory cross-validity, quantile norms and a profile.

Which norms are used?

Separate norms for three education levels: 0 to 5, 6 to 9, and 10 and above years of schooling.

Explain the scoring.

Score responses with the key and manual, add the score for each sub-test, convert each total to a converted score, then use that to get the dysfunction rating. Maximum rating is 3 per sub-test, so 30 for the whole scale.

What does a dysfunction rating of 0 mean?

No dysfunction on that sub-test (converted score 5 or more).

Why does the examiner take special care in sub-tests IV, V, VII and VIII?

Digits, words and pairs must be presented at a controlled rate, and the interval between presenting material and asking for recall must be kept the same, or the memory score is not comparable. extra

Why are the first two sub-tests a concern?

Remote and recent memory are answered from the subject's own life, so the examiner must be careful about the reliability of the answers.

PPSI
What is the PPSI and who made it?

The Psycho-Physiological State Inventory by Sanjay Vohra, an objective psychological test used to assess psychological or physiological dysfunction.

What do Part I and Part II measure?

Part I measures physiological dysfunction. Part II measures psychological dysfunction. The questions are grouped by whether they appear mainly physical or mental.

Who can the PPSI be used with, and by whom?

Late adolescents and adults of either gender. Physicians, clinicians, social workers and school psychologists use it to detect neurotic tendencies.

What are the response options?

Often, Sometimes, Seldom and Never. The subject chooses one of the four.

How long does it take?

Usually about 20 minutes.

Explain the scoring steps.

Use stencils and add the weight of each answer. No number on the key means weight 0. Get Part I, Part II and total raw scores. Subtract 176 from Part I and 249 from Part II. Subtract 425 from the total to get the final score. Convert it to a sten score and a percentile.

Why 176, 249 and 425?

They are the constants in the manual. 176 + 249 = 425, so the total constant is the sum of the two part constants.

What is the Critical Sten Score (CSS)?

The cut-off at 4. Per your journal, a sten below 4 is a serious case and should be referred to a clinician. Check the manual for how a sten exactly at 4 is treated.

What does the percentile mean here?

In your journal a lower sten and percentile mean more dysfunction. A percentile of 40 means about 60 people have less dysfunction and about 39 have more.

What are psychoneurotic disorders?

Mental disorders without any demonstrable organic basis. The person does not confuse morbid subjective experiences and fantasies with external reality. Behaviour stays within socially acceptable limits and personality is not disorganised. Main signs: excessive anxiety, hysterical symptoms, phobias, obsessive and compulsive symptoms, depression.

Limitations of questionnaires like the PPSI?

Limited value because there is no clear-cut clinical description of what a psychoneurotic is, and because of the nature of the test itself. Results depend on the subject's cooperation.

How is the PPSI built?

By empirical procedures with patients rather than logical ones, so it separates patients diagnosed by clinicians from supposedly normal subjects. It can also detect people in the normal group who might benefit from guidance or treatment.

What is the mind-body relation?

Thoughts, emotions, attitudes and beliefs affect blood chemistry, heart rate and the activity of cells and organs, from the digestive tract to the immune system. Looking after our psychological state can prevent illness and speed up recovery.

Name some psycho-physiological methods.

Heart rate and blood pressure; transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), PET, MEG, EEG, structural MRI and fMRI. Questionnaires include the Health Assessment Questionnaire, Gameplay Experience Questionnaire and Berkeley Expressivity Questionnaire.

Where does awareness of the mind-body connection help?

Stress, weight control, smoking cessation, substance abuse, chronic pain and fatigue. It can reduce missed work, speed recovery, reduce pain, shorten hospital stays, help patients and families cope, and raise mental alertness.

EPQ
Who constructed the EPQ?

H.J. Eysenck and Sybil B.G. Eysenck, as a development of the Maudsley Personality Inventory (MPI).

Advantages of the EPQ over the MPI?

It has two parallel forms, A and B, so retesting is possible. It has a lie scale to check social desirability. Items were reworded to suit subjects with less than average intelligence. Items were chosen to remove the small correlation between E and N seen in the MPI. Retest reliability is higher (around 0.85, per your journal) and there is direct evidence of validity.

What are the dimensions?

Psychoticism (P), extraversion-introversion (E), neuroticism-emotional stability (N), plus the lie scale (L).

How many items, and how are they divided?

90 items: 24 psychoticism, 21 extraversion, 24 neuroticism and 21 lie scale.

What is the biological basis of E and N?

Neuroticism is related to the liability of the autonomic nervous system. Extraversion is related to the balance of excitation and inhibition in the central nervous system, mediated by the reticular activating system.

Describe a typical extravert.

Sociable, likes parties, has many friends, needs people to talk to, craves excitement, takes chances, impulsive, loses temper quickly and is not always reliable.

Describe a typical introvert.

Quiet, retiring, introspective, fond of books rather than people, keeps feelings under control, seldom aggressive, does not lose temper easily, reliable, somewhat pessimistic, places great emphasis on ethical standards.

Neurotic vs emotionally stable?

Neurotic: emotionally unstable, easily aroused, moody, touchy, anxious, restless and unreliable. Emotionally stable: less easily aroused, calm, even-tempered, carefree and reliable.

What is psychoticism?

Loss or distortion of reality and inability to tell reality from fantasy, with disturbances in thought, emotion and motor behaviour, delusions or hallucinations. It also covers psychopathy-type traits: anti-social, impulsive, egocentric, absence of guilt.

Is psychoticism a dimension with two poles?

No. Unlike E and N it has no opposite pole in your journal; it is present in varying degrees in individuals.

Name some features of high psychoticism (Eysenck, 1975).

Solitary, troublesome, cruel, insensitive, sensation-seeking, hostile, likes odd things, disregards danger, likes upsetting others, opposed to social customs, little personal interaction. Your journal lists 11.

Psychotic vs neurotic disorders?

Eysenck's studies suggest they are very different, with different routes to each, so a person can become increasingly neurotic without being psychotic.

Name the four temperaments in the E and N quadrants.

Melancholic (introverted, neurotic), choleric (extraverted, neurotic), phlegmatic (introverted, stable), sanguine (extraverted, stable).

What is the lie scale and how do you read it?

An inbuilt device to check answers for social desirability. An average lie score means responses can be taken at face value. A high lie score means social desirability may have shaped the answers, so read the other scores with caution.

Raw score, sten and centile in the EPQ?

Raw is the number of scored items. Sten is a 1 to 10 standard score with mean 5.5 and SD 2. Centile is the percentage of the normal curve below that sten. In your journal sten 1 to 3 is Low, 4 to 7 Average, 8 to 10 High. extra

Why is a raw psychoticism score of 0 not converted?

No sten, centile or interpretation applies to a raw score of 0. It is read as no psychoticism on the EPQ.

How are literate and illiterate subjects handled?

For literates the instructions are printed on the form. For illiterate subjects the questions are read out and the answers recorded.

Why does the plan say high scorers need counselling?

High scores on certain dimensions, such as neuroticism or psychoticism, show tendencies that counselling can help with. extra

Common discussion

7-part skeleton

  1. Who: subject code, age, student.
  2. What the table shows: raw score, then converted score, sten or final score, with real numbers.
  3. Band or rating: Low, Average, High, or dysfunction rating, or sten against the CSS.
  4. Percentile or centile wording: what it means for 100 people.
  5. What it means: one line from the test's theory.
  6. Individual differences (group only) and any lie-score caution.
  7. Conclusion: two or three bullets.

"Out of 100 people, about C score lower and about 99 − C score higher." Example: 89.4 gives 89 lower and 10 higher.
PPSI percentile P
Your journal's rule: "(100 − P) experience less dysfunction and (P − 1) experience more." Example: 40 gives 60 less and 39 more.

No percentile. Use "converted score 5+ indicating no dysfunction", "converted score 4 with a rating of 2 indicating dysfunction".
